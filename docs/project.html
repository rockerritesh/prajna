01 — The everyday problem

Motors fail, usually with warning signs

Electric motors run pumps, fans, conveyors, and machines everywhere. When a bearing wears, a shaft bends, or parts go out of alignment, the motor doesn't stop at once — it first starts vibrating differently. Ignore those signs and you get a sudden breakdown, an expensive repair, and unplanned downtime.

A skilled technician can sometimes feel or hear that "something's off." The goal here is to capture that skill with a cheap sensor and a model, so a fault is flagged early and automatically.

02 — The core idea

Every fault has a vibration fingerprint

A spinning machine vibrates at frequencies tied to its speed and parts. Each fault adds its own telltale pattern: a damaged bearing produces repeated tiny impacts; imbalance shows up strongly at the running speed; misalignment shows at twice the running speed. In the frequency spectrum (the FFT, like in the hero above), these appear as peaks at specific places. The model learns to read those peaks.

In one sentence

Feed the model a short clip of vibration, and it tells you which fault the motor has — like a mechanic diagnosing a machine by the way it shakes and sounds.

03 — The hardware (the bigger half)

A small, real test rig you build

This is where the extra hardware lives. You assemble a bench rig: a motor turning a shaft on a bearing, with sensors watching it. Then you swap in damaged parts to create faults on purpose — that's how you get labeled data.

Small motor + base

A low-power induction or DC motor bolted to a rigid baseplate. The thing whose health you're watching.

core

Shaft, coupling & bearing

The shaft rides in a pillow-block bearing. Swappable so you can fit healthy or damaged bearings.

core

Accelerometer

An ADXL345 or MPU6050 mounted on the bearing housing — measures the vibration. The main sensor.

core sensor

Current sensor

An ACS712 or clamp on the motor supply, so you can add motor-current analysis (MCSA) alongside vibration.

core sensor

MCU / DAQ board

An ESP32 or Arduino with a fast ADC samples the sensors many times a second and streams the data out.

core

Fault specimens

A worn/scratched bearing, an off-centre weight for imbalance, shims for misalignment — your sources of faults.

core

Wiring & supply

Breadboard, jumpers, motor driver/supply, and a USB link to the laptop for power and data.

core

Tachometer (optional)

An IR/hall speed sensor to know exact rpm — helps line faults up with running-speed frequencies.

optional
Safety: a spinning rig has real hazards — add a guard over rotating parts, secure the baseplate, keep fingers and cables clear, and run mains-powered motors only with proper supply protection and your supervisor's sign-off. Start at low speed.

05 — The software

From raw shaking to a named fault

Once the rig produces labelled recordings, this is the AI half: turn the signal into something the model can read, train it, and check how well it tells the faults apart.

Tools

Python · NumPy / SciPy (FFT, spectrograms) · scikit-learn (SVM, Random Forest) · PyTorch or TensorFlow (CNN) · matplotlib for the plots. Firmware in the Arduino IDE for the ESP32.

08 — Where the data comes from

Your rig, backed by public datasets

You don't rely only on your rig. The field has excellent, free, labelled datasets used in most papers — perfect for training and for benchmarking your own results.

Your own rig data

Record healthy and each induced-fault condition. Because you set the fault, every clip is automatically labelled.

CWRU Bearing dataset

The classic Case Western Reserve University bearing data — the standard benchmark for this kind of work.

Paderborn & MAFAULDA

Additional public machinery-fault datasets to broaden training and test how well your model generalises.
